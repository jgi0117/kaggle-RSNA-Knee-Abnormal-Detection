# Fracture

## Scope and use
Literature-derived guidance only; not derived from competition labels.

## Clinical/radiologic concept
MRI is highly sensitive for occult and subchondral fractures because it can show fracture morphology and associated marrow edema-like signal. In acute trauma, MRI findings should be interpreted alongside radiographs and/or CT when bony injury is suspected or characterization is uncertain.

## MRI findings supporting fracture
- Definite fracture line or cortical/trabecular interruption.
- Subchondral insufficiency fracture: characteristic subchondral low-signal line, commonly with surrounding extensive marrow edema-like signal.
- Osteochondral fracture: injury involving subchondral bone and osteochondral surface/fragment.
- Avulsion fracture: osseous fragment at ligament/tendon attachment, often better characterized with radiograph or CT.
- Occult fracture may be MRI-visible despite negative initial radiographs.

## Findings that require caution
- Bone marrow edema-like signal alone is not specific for fracture.
- Bone contusion can produce extensive edema without a definite fracture line.
- Osteonecrosis, osteochondritis dissecans, OA, and stress-related lesions can mimic parts of the subchondral-fracture spectrum.
- Historical “spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee” overlaps with the modern SIFK spectrum.

## Reporting language useful to an LLM
Recognize fracture, insufficiency fracture, stress/fatigue fracture, osteochondral fracture, subchondral fracture, avulsion fracture, and occult fracture. Keep bone bruise/contusion separate unless a fracture component is explicitly described.
